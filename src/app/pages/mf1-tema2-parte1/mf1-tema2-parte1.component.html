Socio-Health Care in Social Institutions (Theme2)

Introduction

Effective socio-health intervention within social institutions requires a structured, collaborative, and person-centered approach. Theme 2 focuses on the operational core of this care: analyzing and executing activities designed to habilitate or rehabilitate the functional capacities of dependent individuals or those with disabilities. This summary outlines the methodologies for user engagement, the protocols for safe mobilization, and the specific competencies of the interdisciplinary team. It emphasizes that the ultimate goal is to enhance the biopsychosocial well-being and autonomy of the user through coordinated, professional execution of individualized care plans.

1. Habilitation/Rehabilitation Activities

Objective: To ensure users engage positively with their environment through individualized care plans.

Key Principles:

  • User-Centered Approach: Activities must be tailored to individual needs, promoting participation to maintain and improve autonomy.
  • Institutional Integration: New users are systematically introduced to the center's organization, including staff, facilities, and schedules, to foster a sense of belonging and facilitate voluntary participation in activities.

2. Mobilization Protocols

Objective: To safely mobilize or transfer users while preserving their physical integrity and ensuring the safety of the healthcare worker.

Key Principles:

  • Safety First: All techniques prioritize preventing user injury or falls and adhere to body mechanics to avoid worker strain.
  • Preparation: Proper preparation involves appropriate attire, non-slip footwear, a hazard-free environment, and clear communication with the user and team members.
  • Purpose: Mobilization aids in preventing complications from prolonged bed rest and supports the user's autonomy in daily activities.

3. Roles & Competencies of the Interdisciplinary Team

Objective: To provide holistic care by integrating diverse professional perspectives, ensuring all user needs (physical, psychological, social) are addressed collaboratively.

Key Team Members:

  • Physician: Conducts medical evaluations, prescribes treatments, oversees rehabilitation programs, and supervises sanitary conditions.
  • Psychologist: Assesses cognitive and emotional health, implements therapies to maintain residual capacities, supports staff communication strategies, and fosters user socialization.
  • Nurse (ATS-DUE): Manages daily healthcare needs, administers medication, monitors vital signs, performs postural changes, and collaborates with physicians and physiotherapists.
  • Social Worker: Evaluates the user's social and family context, manages administrative procedures, coordinates sociocultural activities, and facilitates user integration into the center and community.
  • Physiotherapist: Designs and evaluates rehabilitation treatments, advises on mobilization techniques, and participates in care planning sessions.
  • Occupational Therapist: Develops programs for psychomotor skills, language, and group dynamics to support personal and social rehabilitation.
  • Socio-Cultural Technician (TASOC): Proposes and manages cultural and educational activities to promote user engagement and group development.
  • Socio-Health Assistant (Core Role): Executes the interventions designed by the team across several key areas:
    • Assistential: Direct support with feeding, hygiene, and mobilization.
    • Rehabilitative: Implementing programs for functional recovery (e.g., memory training, mobility).
    • Socializing: Fostering communication, family bonds, and participation in sociocultural events.
    • Educational: Promoting healthy habits and skills for personal independence.

4. Competencies & Responsibilities of the Socio-Health Assistant

The assistant's work is guided by established protocols and focuses on the direct execution of care plans. Their competencies are organized by area of intervention:

  • Nutrition: Assisting with feeding, verifying dietary compliance, and promoting user participation.
  • Hygiene: Collaborating in personal care, performing skin checks, and managing incontinence.
  • Cleaning & Maintenance: Ensuring user rooms and utensils are clean and orderly.
  • Health Care: Monitoring vital signs, identifying anomalies, and applying first aid techniques.
  • Medication: Administering prescribed medication, verifying the "five rights," and observing for adverse effects.
  • Mobilization: Safely performing transfers, ambulation, and postural changes.
  • First Aid: Providing initial emergency care and alerting the medical team.
  • Psychosocial Support: Offering emotional support, accompanying users on outings, and facilitating social interactions.
  • Daily Living Activities (ADLs): Supporting users in both Basic (ABVD: eating, mobility, hygiene) and Instrumental (AIVD: communication, money management) activities of daily living to maximize their independence.
  • Communication: Reporting all relevant observations and incidents to the interdisciplinary team to ensure coordinated and responsive care.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the successful habilitation and rehabilitation of individuals in social institutions depend on a dual foundation: rigorous, person-centered protocols and seamless interdisciplinary collaboration. The socio-health assistant serves as the critical link, translating the strategic plans of medical, psychological, and social professionals into direct, compassionate, and effective daily care. By mastering competencies that range from safe mobilization to psychosocial support and clear communication, these professionals ensure that care is not only assistential but also rehabilitative and empowering. Ultimately, this structured teamwork and specialized execution are what transform institutional living into an environment that actively promotes the dignity, autonomy, and quality of life of every user.
